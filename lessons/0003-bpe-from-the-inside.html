1 · Start from bytes

A byte-level tokenizer first turns text into UTF-8 bytes, so every string becomes a sequence of integers 0–255. You never hit an "unknown word": in the worst case a character is just its bytes. GPT-2 introduced this to remove the out-of-vocabulary problem without needing a vocabulary of all Unicode.Radford 2019 §2.2

Bytes alone make sequences long: "The cat sat on the mat." is 23 bytes. BPE (byte-pair encoding) learns to merge frequent byte sequences into single tokens, and SmolLM2 turns those 23 bytes into 7 tokens. BPE came from compression and was adapted for NMT subwords by Sennrich et al.Sennrich 2016

2 · Training: count pairs, merge the most frequent, repeat

  1. Split the corpus into chunks (§3) and count how often each chunk occurs.
  2. Write each chunk as a sequence of bytes.
  3. Count every adjacent pair of symbols across all chunks, weighted by chunk frequency.
  4. Take the most frequent pair, add it to the vocab as a new symbol, and replace it everywhere.
  5. Repeat until vocab = 256 bytes + #merges + special tokens hits the target.

The output is an ordered list of merges. That order is the tokenizer. SmolLM2's first merges are Ġ t, Ġ a, i n, h e…SmolLM2 tokenizer.json (Ġ is how GPT-2-style tokenizers display the space byte.gpt-2 encoder.py · bytes_to_unicode)

3 · Pre-tokenization: where merges are not allowed

Before BPE runs, a regex splits text into chunks, and merges never cross a chunk boundary. Without it, frequent sequences like dog., dog! and dog? would each become their own token and waste vocab. GPT-2's pattern splits off letters, numbers, punctuation and whitespace, with a leading space staying attached to the word after it.gpt-2 encoder.py

SmolLM2 runs two pre-tokenizers in sequence: Digits(individual_digits=true), then ByteLevel(add_prefix_space=false) with the GPT-2 regex. In practice:tokenizer.json

Two things to remember. (1) The leading space belongs to the token: hello and Ġhello are different IDs. (2) Every digit is its own token, so numbers cost one token per digit.

4 · Encoding: replay the merges in order

To encode new text: pre-tokenize, turn each chunk into bytes, then repeatedly apply whichever applicable merge was learned earliest until none applies. It is not "longest match in the vocab". Two tokenizers with the same vocab but different merge orders can split the same word differently.

Exercise 3SmolLM2's vocab has 49,152 entries: 17 special tokens and 235 single-byte tokens. Everything else is a merge. How many merges?

49,152 − 17 − 235 = 48,900, which matches len(merges) in tokenizer.json. (Only 235 of 256 bytes are in the vocab, which is a small departure from "every byte is a token".)

Exercise 4Why splice reflection token IDs in, rather than insert the reflection text into the document and re-tokenize?

Inserting text changes chunk boundaries next to the insertion point: a leading space can move, or a word can get split. The document's own tokens would then differ from the Vanilla run's. Splicing keeps them identical, so SPP vs Vanilla stays data-matched.

Exercise 5Why do hello and hello get different IDs?

The regex attaches the leading space to the word's chunk, so " hello" merges into one token that includes the space. A reflection tokenized alone starts with no leading space, e.g. I (57) rather than ĠI. That's fine, as long as your Levanter port tokenizes it exactly the same way.

Exercise 6How does encoding decide which merges to apply?

Merge rank = training order. Encoding replays training's decisions on new text.
